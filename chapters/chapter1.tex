\subsection{Background}
\label{sec:into_back}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Before diving into the core issue of the topic, it is useful to provide some
background information. The team I worked with during this internship was called
``Digital Government'' --- which directly reflects the primary objective this
project aims to serve.

Digital government services --- also referred to as e-government --- refer to
the use of information and communication technologies to improve the efficiency,
effectiveness, and transparency of government operations. These systems reduce
bureaucratic delays, minimise paperwork, and enhance the overall quality of
public services.

E-government systems are commonly categorised based on the type of interaction
they facilitate. Three main categories exist: Government-to-Government (G2G),
Government-to-Citizen (G2C), and Government-to-Employee (G2E).

G2G refers to interactions between different government agencies or departments,
whether at the same level or across levels of government. An example relevant to
this project is the sharing of agricultural satellite data between a national
space agency and a ministry of agriculture to inform food security decisions.

G2C focuses on delivering government services and information directly to
citizens --- making them more accessible, timely, and actionable. In the context
of agriculture, this translates to providing smallholder farmers and local
administrations with accurate, up-to-date crop production intelligence.

G2E refers to internal government process management and employee communication
--- for example, equipping field agents with data-driven tools to support their
monitoring activities.

The Digital Government team at Oracle aims to leverage existing government data
and modern technologies to solve real-world problems across sectors including
agriculture, healthcare, and education. This project sits firmly within the
agricultural domain and contributes to Oracle's Government Data Intelligence for
Agriculture platform --- launched in September 2025 --- which supports
governments in building more resilient and data-driven food systems.

It is important to note that this project is part of an ongoing collaboration
with different governments. The requirements and needs of each country differ,
and Rwanda represents one of the early adopters of this approach.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

\label{sec:intro_prob_art}

% ------------------------------------------------------------

Agriculture is central to Rwanda's economy and national food security,
employing a large share of the population and supporting rural livelihoods.
For governments and development organisations to make effective decisions on
resource allocation, input distribution, early warning of food shortfalls, and
seasonal crop production estimates, they need timely and spatially detailed
information about what crops are growing, where they are located, and at what
scale. In practice, this information remains difficult to obtain. National crop
inventories in Rwanda and across sub-Saharan Africa still depend heavily on
ground surveys, which are costly, spatially sparse, time-consuming, and often
available only after the period when early intervention would have been most
useful.

Satellite remote sensing offers a practical way to reduce this information
gap. Sentinel-2, the European Space Agency's open-access multispectral
satellite constellation, provides repeated observations over Rwanda at
ten-metre spatial resolution throughout the growing season. However, the
availability of satellite imagery alone is not sufficient. A reliable and
reproducible machine learning pipeline is needed to transform raw Sentinel-2
time series into accurate crop type maps that can support field-level
agricultural monitoring.

Developing such a pipeline is challenging in the Rwandan context. Smallholder
fields are often small and fragmented, so some Sentinel-2 pixels may capture
mixed spectral signals from multiple neighbouring crops. Persistent cloud cover
during the growing season creates temporal gaps in the satellite record, which
must be handled before meaningful analysis can be performed. Crop datasets are
also strongly imbalanced: majority crops such as maize and rice greatly
outnumber minority crops such as bean and Irish potato, which can cause
standard classifiers to favour dominant classes. In addition, crop phenology,
including green-up, peak growth, and senescence, contains important
discriminative information that simple per-date spectral features may not
capture. Finally, it remains necessary to compare classical machine learning
models with deep learning sequence models to determine which approach is most
appropriate for this crop mapping task.

This project addresses these challenges by designing, implementing, and
evaluating an end-to-end crop type mapping workflow for Rwanda. The work is
aligned with the Digital Government team's mission to provide governments with
the data intelligence needed to build more resilient and evidence-based food
systems.

\subsection{Aims and Objectives}

\label{sec:intro_aims_obj}

% ------------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection*{Aim}

The aim of this project is to develop and evaluate a reproducible machine
learning workflow for multi-class crop type mapping in Rwanda using
Sentinel-2 satellite time series and field-based ground-truth data. The
workflow is intended to support agricultural monitoring by producing crop
classification results that are technically reliable, interpretable, and
relevant to government decision-making.

\subsubsection*{Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Prepare the ground-truth and satellite dataset.}
    Validate the Harvard Dataverse Rwanda crop mapping ground-truth data,
    align it with Rwanda's Season~B growing window, and use the joined
    Sentinel-2 time series to construct a QUADKEY-level dataset suitable for
    supervised crop classification.

    \item \textbf{Design and implement a multi-stage quality control pipeline.}
    Address the main data quality challenges affecting satellite-based crop
    mapping, including duplicate observations, invalid or irregular polygon
    geometries, cloud and aerosol contamination, temporal duplicates,
    boundary-pixel label conflicts, and missing spectral observations.

    \item \textbf{Engineer a rich, physically meaningful feature representation.}
    Compute vegetation indices from Sentinel-2 reflectance bands and extract
    statistical and phenological descriptors that capture crop greenness,
    moisture, canopy structure, chlorophyll sensitivity, seasonal timing, and
    growth dynamics.

    \item \textbf{Address class imbalance during model development.}
    Compare cost-sensitive learning and synthetic oversampling strategies,
    including class weighting, SMOTE-based variants, and ADASYN, while applying
    resampling strictly inside cross-validation folds to prevent data leakage.

    \item \textbf{Train and compare classical and deep learning models.}
    Benchmark classical machine learning classifiers on the engineered feature
    matrix and compare them with deep learning sequence models trained directly
    on the temporal Sentinel-2 observations.

    \item \textbf{Evaluate model performance using robust protocols.}
    Assess model performance using metrics suitable for imbalanced
    multi-class classification, with macro F1 as the primary metric, and report
    results under both the main QUADKEY-level split and a field/group-level
    robustness check for spatial leakage.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Solution Approach}
\label{sec:intro_sol}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The solution is an end-to-end crop type classification pipeline that
reads satellite imagery and produces a map showing what crop is growing
in each field across Rwanda --- without requiring a single field survey.

\paragraph{From raw satellite data to a clean time series.}
Sentinel-2 satellites pass over Rwanda every five days, capturing the
spectral reflectance of every field at ten-metre resolution. However,
raw satellite data is far from ready to use: cloud cover blocks the
view on many dates, multiple satellite passes produce duplicate
observations for the same location, and field boundaries often fall
across more than one crop type. A rigorous data cleaning process was
designed to handle all of these issues systematically, producing a
clean, complete seasonal time series for each pixel across the
study area.

\paragraph{Teaching the model what each crop looks like.}
Different crops grow differently throughout the season --- maize rises
quickly to a high peak and then drops sharply, while beans grow more
slowly and plateau earlier. These seasonal patterns, visible in the
satellite signal, are what the model learns to distinguish between
crop types. To capture them, a rich set of features was extracted
from each pixel's time series --- describing not just the average
spectral signal, but also its shape, timing, and intensity across
the growing season.

\paragraph{Handling the imbalance between crop types.}
In Rwanda, maize and rice fields far outnumber bean and Irish potato
fields. Without corrective measures, a machine learning model trained
on this data would learn to recognise maize and rice well but largely
ignore the minority crops. Several strategies were tested and compared
to ensure that the model gives equal attention to all four crop types,
regardless of how frequently they appear in the training data.

\paragraph{Comparing model approaches.}
Both classical machine learning models --- which operate on the
extracted seasonal features --- and modern deep learning models ---
which learn directly from the raw time series --- were trained and
evaluated. This comparison provides insight into whether the manual
feature engineering step adds value over letting the model discover
temporal patterns on its own.

\paragraph{Contribution.}
This project shows that it is possible to produce accurate, national-scale
crop type maps from freely available satellite data, without sending a
single surveyor into the field. The resulting pipeline is reproducible,
openly documented, and directly integrable into Oracle's Government
Data Intelligence for Agriculture platform --- providing a practical
blueprint for scaling this approach to other countries, other seasons,
and other crops.

\section{Project Management}
\label{sec:proj_mgmt}
% ============================================================

This section presents the organisational framework followed during the
internship. The project combined research, experimentation, implementation,
and reporting activities, and was therefore managed through progressive
technical milestones, regular follow-up meetings, and continuous feedback from
the Digital Government team.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Work Organisation}
\label{subsec:work_organisation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The internship began with an onboarding and preparation phase, during which I
became familiar with Oracle's internal environment, completed the required
compliance training, configured my working setup, and gained access to the
tools needed for the project.

The work then moved into a research and exploration phase focused on remote
sensing, Sentinel-2 satellite imagery, crop monitoring, and machine learning
for agricultural applications. This phase helped define the technical
foundation of the project before the implementation of the crop classification
pipelines.

The technical work was carried out in successive stages. An initial pipeline
was first developed using the Oracle dataset, which helped identify important
data-quality and modelling challenges. After the team presentation milestone,
the workflow was refined and applied to the Harvard Dataverse dataset, which
became the main dataset used in the final methodology and results. Report
writing was conducted in parallel with the final technical stages.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Communication and Collaboration}
\label{subsec:communication_collaboration}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Regular communication was essential for keeping the project aligned with the
team's expectations. Weekly synchronisation meetings with my mentor were used
to review progress, discuss methodological choices, resolve technical issues,
and define the next priorities.

In addition, weekly team meetings were held to share progress, discuss current
tasks, clarify priorities, and receive feedback from other team members.

Several tools supported the collaboration process. \textbf{Zoom} was used for
remote meetings, mentor follow-ups, and the project presentation.
\textbf{Confluence} was used to organise project documentation, research notes,
technical references, and decisions made during the internship.
\textbf{Jira} was used mainly during onboarding and training to track access,
setup, and administrative tickets. \textbf{Slack} supported day-to-day
communication, quick technical questions, and the sharing of intermediate
updates with team members. The technical implementation was carried out mainly
in \textbf{OCI JupyterLab}, which was used for data exploration,
preprocessing, modelling, and figure generation.
