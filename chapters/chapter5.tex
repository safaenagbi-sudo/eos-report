\chapter{Discussion and Analysis}
\label{ch:discussion}

This chapter interprets and analyses the results presented in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. Each section follows the same order as the
pipeline: it explains what the findings mean, why they occurred, and
how they relate to the research objectives and the existing literature.
The significance of the work for food security intelligence is then
assessed, the limitations are identified, and the chapter closes with
a summary that transitions to the conclusions.


% ============================================================
\section{Interpretation of Results}
\label{sec:interpretation}
% ============================================================

The subsections below address each stage of the pipeline in turn.
The goal is not to restate the numbers reported in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, but to explain what those numbers reveal
about the data, the methodology, and the crop classification problem
in Rwanda.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Preprocessing and Feature Engineering}
\label{subsec:disc_preprocessing}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The preprocessing pipeline reduced 1,788,552 raw observations to
21,865 clean QUADKEYs. The most revealing reduction was full-row
deduplication, which removed 502,800 rows --- 33.7\% of the temporally
filtered dataset. This high duplication rate is a direct consequence
of the spatial join process: when a ground-truth polygon overlaps
multiple Sentinel-2 tile export boundaries, the same observation is
delivered more than once. At this scale, deduplication is not merely
good practice but a correctness requirement. Retaining duplicates
would artificially inflate the dataset, introduce correlated samples
into cross-validation folds, and bias training toward over-represented
spatial regions.

\paragraph{Feature correlation and redundancy.}
The 96-feature matrix combines 60 statistical and 36 phenological
descriptors. The high inter-feature correlation confirmed in the
heatmap was expected: indices sharing spectral bands encode partially
overlapping biophysical information. This redundancy is not a flaw
in the feature engineering design; it is a consequence of using
physically meaningful indices that happen to be correlated. It does,
however, make a feature selection step necessary before model
training, as retaining all 96 features would introduce redundancy
without adding discriminative power.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Feature Selection}
\label{subsec:disc_feature_selection}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The finding that all 96 features passed the Bonferroni-corrected
ANOVA filter confirms that every engineered variable carries at
least some class-discriminative signal. However, individual
statistical significance does not imply non-redundancy. Two features
can each be significantly associated with class membership while
still encoding the same underlying biophysical dimension. The ANOVA
stage therefore served primarily as a quality confirmation ---
ruling out completely uninformative features --- rather than as a
dimensionality reduction step.

The dominance of moisture and dryness indices in the Random Forest
importance ranking has a clear agronomic explanation. Paddy rice
cultivation in Rwanda relies on flooded or waterlogged paddies,
producing a persistently low LSWI and NBR signature throughout the
growing season that is absent in dryland crops such as maize and
beans. The seasonal minimum values of these indices are therefore
particularly discriminative: a very low NBR\_min or LSWI\_min
reliably identifies rice, while maize and beans maintain higher
minimum values. This is consistent with the established use of
SWIR-based moisture indices for paddy rice
mapping~\cite{xiao2005}.

Reducing from 96 to 37 features via RFECV removed redundant index
variants --- particularly the multiple NDRE formulations
(NDRE\_B06, NDRE\_B07, NDRE\_B8A\_B05), which encode similar
red-edge information at slightly different wavelengths --- while
retaining the most discriminative statistical and phenological
descriptors. The result is a leaner feature set that trains faster,
generalises better, and is directly interpretable in agronomic
terms.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Model Benchmarking and Selection}
\label{subsec:disc_benchmarking}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The 0.10-point macro F1 gap between tree-based ensembles and Linear
SVM confirms that crop-type boundaries in the 37-feature space are
substantially non-linear. The spectral and phenological differences
between maize, beans, irish potato, and rice do not follow simple
linear separating hyperplanes, particularly given the within-class
variability driven by different planting dates, field management
practices, and soil conditions across four heterogeneous districts.
Non-linear ensemble methods are therefore the appropriate model
family for this problem.

\paragraph{Why macro F1 was chosen over balanced accuracy and G-mean.}
Macro F1 was selected as the primary model-selection metric because
it accounts for both precision and recall through their harmonic
mean. Balanced accuracy and G-mean are recall-only metrics: they
reward a model for correctly identifying true positive samples but
do not penalise it for false positives. A model that aggressively
predicts minority classes to achieve high recall on BEAN and IRISH
POTATO would score well on balanced accuracy while producing many
false positives. For an operational crop map, false positives are
as costly as false negatives --- incorrectly labelling a maize field
as bean produces the same error in area estimation as failing to
detect a real bean field. Macro F1 penalises both types of error
equally and is therefore the more appropriate operational metric.

Under this criterion, Extra Trees ranked first (mean CV macro
F1~=~0.9159) ahead of XGBoost (0.9149) and Random Forest (0.8945),
and was correctly selected as the lead candidate for hyperparameter
tuning.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Class Imbalance Handling}
\label{subsec:disc_imbalance}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The selection of per-class SMOTE medium reflects a calibrated
trade-off between two failure modes: under-representation, where
the model sees too few minority-class examples to learn reliable
decision boundaries; and over-generation, where an excess of
synthetic samples dominates the learned boundary in a way that does
not reflect the true data distribution.

Class weighting only (average rank 5.33) addressed neither problem:
it adjusts the loss function but does not expand the feature space
coverage for minority classes. With only 641--814 real training
QUADKEYs for BEAN and IRISH POTATO, the model lacks the variety of
real examples needed to generalise reliably to unseen minority-class
observations regardless of how heavily errors on those classes are
penalised.

Full SMOTE (average rank 5.00) fell into the opposite failure mode.
Oversampling to the MAIZE count of 9,843 would generate approximately
9,000 synthetic samples per minority class --- more than eleven times
the number of real examples --- risking synthetic samples dominating
the learned decision boundary in regions of the feature space that
do not correspond to real crop spectral signatures.

Per-class SMOTE medium generated approximately 2,902 synthetic BEAN
samples and 3,075 synthetic IRISH POTATO samples, tripling the
effective minority-class size while keeping synthetic samples in a
ratio to real ones that avoids manifold distortion. The consistent
ranking of this strategy across all three top model families confirms
that the balance is genuinely better, not an artefact of one
particular model's preference.

ADASYN ranked last (average rank 6.33) because its adaptive
allocation of synthetic samples relies on accurate boundary
estimation between classes. With only 641--814 real minority-class
samples, boundary estimation is unreliable, causing ADASYN to place
synthetic samples in suboptimal regions of the feature space.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Final Model Performance}
\label{subsec:disc_final_performance}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The tuned Extra Trees model achieved a macro F1 of 0.9305 on the
untouched test set. The result obtained here falls within the
upper range of values reported in comparable Sentinel-2 crop-mapping
studies, although direct comparison is limited by differences in crop
classes, spatial resolution, sampling design, and class imbalance.

\paragraph{Why RICE is the easiest class and BEAN the hardest.}
RICE achieved the highest F1 (0.9948) because paddy cultivation
produces a distinctive and consistent spectral and temporal
signature: persistently high canopy water content, flooded soil
background during transplanting, and a temporal trajectory of
green-up and senescence that differs markedly from dryland crops.
The moisture and SWIR indices that dominate the feature importance
ranking capture this signature directly, making rice the most
separable class in the 37-feature space.

BEAN achieved the lowest recall (0.8039). Two factors explain this.
First, beans and maize have overlapping spectral and phenological
signatures in the 37-feature space, particularly in their
mid-season vegetative growth phase. Second, with only 814 training
QUADKEYs --- the smallest real sample size of the four classes ---
the model has limited coverage of the true bean feature
distribution, making it more likely to assign ambiguous bean
observations to the neighbouring maize cluster.
BEAN$\rightarrow$MAIZE was the dominant misclassification (27 cases,
29\% of all errors), directly reflecting this spectral proximity.

\paragraph{ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC for minority classes.}
The gap between BEAN's ROC-AUC (0.9958) and PR-AUC (0.9447) is
the largest among the four classes. ROC-AUC is computed from the
full confusion matrix: in a one-vs-rest evaluation for BEAN, the
model correctly rejects the vast majority of non-bean samples,
inflating the true-negative rate and producing an apparently high
score. PR-AUC is computed only from positive-class predictions and
is unaffected by the large true-negative pool. The 0.055-point gap
confirms that ROC-AUC substantially overstates BEAN classification
quality, and that PR-AUC is the more informative and honest metric
for minority classes in imbalanced crop classification tasks.
Studies reporting only ROC-AUC for such settings may present
optimistic minority-class performance estimates.

\paragraph{Why Extra Trees was used for inference despite the 1D
CNN achieving a higher macro F1.}
The 1D CNN achieved a macro F1 of 0.9324, which is 0.0019 points
above the tuned Extra Trees result. Despite this, Extra Trees was
used for the Nyagatare district inference for three reasons.

The first is pipeline design and protocol integrity. The model
selection pipeline was built
specifically for classical tabular models operating on the
37-feature matrix. The 1D CNN operates on a fundamentally different
input format: a 3D sequence tensor of shape
$(N \times 30 \times 29)$. Including it in the same
cross-validation selection loop would have required building a
separate pipeline, which was outside the scope of the original
design. The 1D CNN was therefore evaluated as a complementary
sequence-model benchmark after the classical model-selection
decision was already made. Using the 1D CNN test result to reverse
the selection would mean choosing a model based on held-out test
information, which is precisely what the strict evaluation protocol
is designed to prevent.

The second is that the performance difference is not practically
meaningful. A gap of 0.0019 macro F1 falls well within the
fold-to-fold variance observed during cross-validation benchmarking
(standard deviation of 0.0114 for Extra Trees across five folds).

The third is operational. The production environment used by the
Government Data Intelligence for Agriculture platform runs on CPU
only. Extra Trees inference
on 4,088,243~QUADKEYs requires only a 37-feature tabular matrix
and completes in minutes using parallelised tree evaluation. The
1D CNN requires constructing and processing a sequence tensor of
shape $4{,}088{,}243 \times 30 \times 29$ through multiple
convolutional layers on CPU, imposing prohibitive latency at
district scale and making national-scale deployment across Rwanda's
30~districts entirely impractical without GPU infrastructure.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Sequence Models}
\label{subsec:disc_sequence}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The 1D CNN result --- macro F1 of 0.9324 without any hand-crafted
feature engineering --- is the most theoretically interesting
finding of this study. It demonstrates that the raw 30-timestep
Sentinel-2 sequence contains sufficient discriminative temporal
structure for strong crop-type classification, and that
convolutional filters applied along the time dimension can extract
this structure automatically.

The 1D CNN outperforming both LSTM and GRU on macro F1 suggests
that local temporal patterns are more discriminative than long-range
sequential dependencies for this problem. The key events that
distinguish crops --- the flooded transplanting window for rice,
the rapid green-up for maize, the slower canopy development for
beans --- are relatively short and localised in the 30-week
Season~B window. A convolutional filter with kernel size 3 captures
these local transitions directly, without the overhead of a
recurrent hidden state that tracks dependencies across the full
sequence.

The higher balanced accuracy of LSTM (0.9664) and GRU (0.9615)
relative to the 1D CNN (0.9575) indicates that recurrent models
are slightly better at identifying minority classes through
sequential context. This advantage in recall comes at the cost of
slightly lower majority-class precision, producing a lower macro
F1 overall.

For future work, a hyperparameter-tuned 1D CNN operating on raw
Sentinel-2 sequences may eliminate the feature engineering step
entirely while matching or exceeding the classical pipeline's
performance --- provided that GPU inference becomes available in
the production environment.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Spatial Robustness}
\label{subsec:disc_spatial}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The field/group split is a research robustness check designed to
assess whether the QUADKEY-level performance is inflated by spatial
autocorrelation within the training data. In this split, all
QUADKEYs belonging to the same source ground-truth polygon are
kept in the same partition, so the model cannot see QUADKEYs from
the same labelled polygon in both training and test. This is not a
simulation of operational deployment: in production, inference is
performed on individual satellite pixels without any knowledge of
field boundaries, because field delineations do not exist for
unlabelled areas and discovering them would require a separate
field delineation step, defeating the purpose of an automated
satellite pipeline.

The 0.11-point drop in macro F1 from the QUADKEY-level split
(0.9277 for Extra Trees) to the field/group split (0.8136) reveals
that part of the QUADKEY-level performance is driven by spatial
autocorrelation. Neighbouring QUADKEYs from the same labelled
polygon share the same crop label, soil conditions, planting date,
and annotation source. A model evaluated on these neighbours
benefits from implicit proximity in feature space to training
samples it has already seen. The QUADKEY-level macro F1 of 0.9277
is therefore an optimistic estimate. The field/group split reduces
this effect and produces the more conservative estimate of 0.8136.

The finding that XGBoost generalises better than Extra Trees under
the field/group split (macro F1: 0.8227 vs 0.8136; balanced
accuracy: 0.8323 vs 0.8012) is consistent with XGBoost's stronger
minority-class sensitivity in the CV benchmark (balanced accuracy
0.9368 vs 0.8811). This suggests that for applications where
generalisation to unseen labelled polygons is the priority,
XGBoost may be the preferable model despite ranking second on the
QUADKEY-level test.

\subsection{Nyagatare District Inference and Validation}
\label{subsec:disc_inference}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The inference map was assessed through two complementary approaches:
visual spatial plausibility using QGIS and high-resolution basemap
imagery, and a cross-check against the NISR Seasonal Agricultural
Survey Season~B~2025~\cite{nisr2025sas}.

\paragraph{Visual plausibility.}
The Dynamic World cropland mask correctly excluded the built-up
areas visible in Figure~\ref{fig:crop_mask}, confirming that the
mask prevents crop predictions over non-agricultural surfaces. The
RICE predictions shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rice1} form spatially
coherent clusters on areas exhibiting the visual characteristics
of irrigated paddy cultivation: flat terrain, regular rectangular
plot boundaries, and surface reflectance consistent with flooded
or waterlogged fields. This spatial alignment is consistent with
Rwanda's paddy rice cultivation system, concentrated in
valley-bottom wetland areas, and with the SAS Season~B~2025
identification of Nyagatare as one of Rwanda's top paddy rice
producing districts~\cite{nisr2025sas}.

\paragraph{MAIZE dominance and SAS consistency.}
The SAS Season~B~2025 identifies Nyagatare as the highest maize
producing district in the Eastern Province~\cite{nisr2025sas}.
The inference map's prediction that 85.1\% of cropland QUADKEYs
are MAIZE is directionally consistent with this characterisation.
The predicted spatial dominance of maize reflects the real
agricultural composition of the district as documented by the
official survey.

\paragraph{Bean discrepancy and the intercropping explanation.}
The most significant divergence between the inference map and the
SAS concerns BEAN. The SAS reports a cultivated bean area of
29,778~Ha in Nyagatare (Table~9~\cite{nisr2025sas}), while the
inference map predicts BEAN for only 4.9\% of cropland QUADKEYs.
This divergence is structurally expected and should not be interpreted
automatically as model failure.

The SAS Season~B~2025 (Table~15) reports that \textbf{75.9\% of
cultivated area in Nyagatare is under intercropping} --- the
highest rate in the Eastern Province (national average:
59.9\%)~\cite{nisr2025sas}. Bean cultivation in the district is
predominantly practised as intercrop with maize, where bean plants
grow between maize rows on the same plot. The SAS counts such
plots under both maize and bean area simultaneously, because it
measures what is physically planted regardless of whether crops
share a field.

The model was trained exclusively on monocrop-filtered ground-truth
labels --- pure maize signatures and pure bean signatures learned
separately. At inference time in Nyagatare, the model encounters
a landscape where the majority of cultivated area is intercropped.
When beans are intercropped with maize on the same plot, the 10~m
pixel contains a mixed spectral signal that the model has never
been trained on. The model assigns the label of whichever pure
signature the mixed pixel most closely resembles. Because maize
grows taller than beans and its leaf area index is larger, it
dominates the canopy reflectance of the intercropped pixel. The
model therefore predicts MAIZE --- the spectrally correct answer
given what it was trained to recognise, even if it is
agronomically incomplete at the field level.

The divergence between the SAS bean area and the inference map
is therefore a measurement mismatch, not a classification error.
The SAS counts all crops planted on a field regardless of whether
they share it; the model classifies the dominant spectral signal
in each 10~m pixel. These are fundamentally different quantities
in a high-intercropping agricultural system.

\paragraph{The uncertain category as a quality indicator.}
The 319,612~QUADKEYs labelled as \texttt{uncertain} (7.8\% of
all cropland QUADKEYs) represent predictions where the model's
class probability did not meet the class-specific confidence
threshold. This category is a transparency feature: it identifies
the areas of the district where the model is least confident and
flags them for potential field verification, rather than silently
forcing a label on every pixel regardless of confidence.

\section{Significance of the Findings}
\label{sec:significance}
% ============================================================


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Technical Significance}
\label{subsec:sig_technical}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

This study demonstrates that a classical ensemble classifier with
engineered phenological and statistical features from Sentinel-2
time series achieves competitive crop-type classification
performance (macro F1~=~0.9305) for a four-class imbalanced
problem in a smallholder farming landscape. The result is achieved
through a reproducible, modular pipeline that is operational at
district scale on CPU-only infrastructure. The three-stage feature
selection cascade --- ANOVA with Bonferroni correction, Random
Forest importance ranking, and RFECV --- provides a transferable
design pattern for feature reduction in remote sensing
classification tasks where redundant vegetation indices are common.

The 1D CNN result (macro F1~=~0.9324 without any hand-crafted
feature engineering) is significant in its own right. It shows
that convolutional filters applied directly to the raw Sentinel-2
temporal sequence can match or exceed the performance of a heavily
engineered and tuned classical pipeline. This has practical
implications for future work: a well-tuned 1D CNN may eliminate
the feature engineering step entirely while achieving equal or
superior accuracy, provided GPU inference infrastructure becomes
available in the production environment.

The ROC-AUC versus PR-AUC gap for BEAN (0.9958 vs 0.9447)
constitutes a methodological finding with implications for how
crop classification studies should report minority-class
performance. PR-AUC is the more informative metric for imbalanced
classes and should be reported alongside ROC-AUC in future remote
sensing classification work.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Practical Significance for Food Security}
\label{subsec:sig_practical}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

This study forms part of Oracle's Government Data Intelligence
for Agriculture platform, an end-to-end food security intelligence
system developed in collaboration with the Rwanda Space Agency and
the Ministry of Agriculture and Animal Resources (MINAGRI). Within
this platform, crop type mapping constitutes the foundational
spatial layer: it identifies what is grown and where at 10~m
field resolution, providing the input that downstream components
use for yield estimation and production forecasting.

The contribution to the food security chain operates at three
levels.

At the \textbf{estimation} level, the district-scale crop type
map provides spatially explicit cultivated area estimates at 10~m
resolution for every crop across the district, complementing the
NISR Seasonal Agricultural Survey which produces district-level
aggregates from a sample of field segments~\cite{nisr2025sas}.
Unlike the survey, the satellite-derived map covers every
cropland pixel in the district without sampling.

At the \textbf{forecasting} level, the crop type map is produced
at the end of Season~B --- after the full February--August time
series is available but before the harvest is fully concluded.
This end-of-season map provides input to the platform's yield
and production models, giving policymakers an early signal of
district-level crop composition before harvest volumes are
confirmed. It also informs planning for the following Season~A:
knowing what was grown in Season~B helps MINAGRI advise farmers
on crop rotation decisions and anticipate seed and fertiliser
demand for the next planting cycle. A future extension of the
pipeline using only early-season observations could enable
genuinely mid-season forecasting, extending the operational
lead time further ahead of harvest.

At the \textbf{targeting} level, field-scale crop type
information enables MINAGRI to direct agricultural support to
the specific locations where a given crop is actually grown,
rather than distributing resources uniformly across a whole
district based on survey averages. For example, if the map
shows that rice is concentrated in the valley bottoms of eastern
Nyagatare, irrigation investment can be directed there.
Extension agents can visit the right villages. Subsidised seeds
and fertiliser can reach the farmers who grow the relevant crop.
This sub-district targeting improves the efficiency and impact
of agricultural support programmes.

The pipeline is particularly relevant for Rwanda's smallholder
farming system. At coarser resolutions such as Landsat (30~m) or
MODIS (250~m), multiple smallholder fields averaging 0.1--0.5~Ha
are aggregated into a single pixel, making individual field-level
classification impossible. At 10~m, a 0.1~Ha field spans
approximately 10 pixels, making smallholder fields detectable
for the first time at this resolution. While 10~m does not
provide precise field boundary delineation, it is sufficient to
identify the dominant crop at sub-field scale and to produce
district-level crop maps at a spatial granularity that is
meaningfully finer than survey-based estimates.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Scientific Significance}
\label{subsec:sig_scientific}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The intercropping finding has implications beyond this study.
In Nyagatare, where 75.9\% of cultivated area is
intercropped~\cite{nisr2025sas}, farmer-reported cultivated
area statistics and pixel-level spectral classification are
measuring fundamentally different quantities. The SAS counts
all crops planted on a field; the satellite classifier
identifies the dominant spectral signal in each 10~m pixel.
In a high-intercropping system, these two measurements diverge
structurally --- not because one is wrong, but because they
answer different questions.

Any study that compares remote sensing crop area estimates
against official agricultural statistics in sub-Saharan Africa
without accounting for intercropping prevalence risks
misinterpreting a measurement mismatch as model failure. The
quantified intercropping rate from the SAS makes this a
reproducible and verifiable finding rather than a speculative
argument, and it applies broadly to any pixel-level crop
mapping study conducted in intercropped agricultural landscapes
across the region.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ============================================================

The following limitations should be considered when interpreting
the results and assessing the generalisability of the pipeline.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Single Season and Temporal Generalisation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The model was trained exclusively on Season~B~2025 data. Rwanda
has three agricultural seasons with different crop compositions
and phenological calendars. Performance on Season~A or in future
years has not been assessed. Whether a model trained on 2025
spectral signatures generalises reliably to 2026 data --- given
inter-annual variability in rainfall onset, planting dates, and
crop development --- remains an open question.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Geographic Scope}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Training data were collected from four districts covering
different agro-ecological zones: Eastern lowlands (Nyagatare),
Southern plateau (Ruhango), Northern volcanic highlands (Musanze),
and Western highlands (Nyabihu). These do not represent all of
Rwanda's agricultural diversity. Transfer to districts outside
the training coverage may degrade performance, particularly for
crops with strong agro-ecological specificity such as Irish
potato, which is concentrated in highland zones.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Spatial Leakage in the Primary Evaluation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The QUADKEY-level macro F1 of 0.9305 is an optimistic estimate
of generalisation due to spatial autocorrelation between
neighbouring QUADKEYs from the same labelled polygon. The
field/group macro F1 of 0.8136 is the more conservative estimate
that reduces the influence of this autocorrelation. The
QUADKEY-level result should be interpreted as an upper bound.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Intercropping}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The model was trained on monocrop-filtered ground-truth labels
and cannot identify intercropped fields. In practice, 75.9\%
of Nyagatare's cultivated area is intercropped. The model
predicts the dominant spectral signal in each pixel, which is
a well-defined and interpretable output, but it does not account
for secondary crops growing on the same plot.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Inference Validation and Ground Truth}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The inference map was not validated against spatially explicit
field-level ground truth for Nyagatare. No labelled field
polygons for Nyagatare's unlabelled cropland areas were available
within the scope of this study. The Harvard Dataverse dataset
provides labelled polygons for Nyagatare, but all of these were
used in model training and testing and cannot serve as independent
validation for the inference map. Acquiring new field-collected
ground truth is the primary missing requirement for a quantitative spatial
accuracy assessment. The two validation approaches used in this
study --- visual spatial plausibility assessment and cross-check
against the NISR SAS Season~B~2025 statistics --- are indicative
rather than rigorous.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Sequence Model Tuning}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The 1D CNN, LSTM, and GRU models were evaluated with default
architectures and training parameters. None were subjected to
hyperparameter tuning comparable to the
\texttt{RandomizedSearchCV} applied to the classical models.
The sequence model results therefore represent a conservative
lower bound on what these architectures could achieve with
proper tuning.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Temporal Resolution}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The 7-day compositing window may miss fast phenological events
--- such as the flooded transplanting window for paddy rice,
which can last only a few days --- that would be captured by
daily or near-daily observations. Fusing optical Sentinel-2
with Sentinel-1 SAR data could improve discrimination for crops
with distinctive radar backscatter signatures.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:discussion-summary}
% ============================================================

This chapter interpreted the results of the crop type
classification pipeline developed in this study. The dominance
of moisture and dryness indices in feature importance was
explained through the distinctive wetland spectral signature
of paddy rice. Macro F1 was justified as the primary selection
metric over recall-only alternatives. Per-class SMOTE medium
was explained as the optimal balance between minority-class
under-representation and synthetic over-generation. The
ROC-AUC versus PR-AUC gap for BEAN was identified as a
methodological finding relevant to how future crop
classification studies should report minority-class performance.

Extra Trees was retained for district-scale inference despite
the 1D CNN achieving a marginally higher macro F1, on three
grounds: the 1D CNN was evaluated outside the model selection
pipeline and its result was only observed after the test set
was opened; the 0.0019 macro F1 gap is within CV fold variance
and not practically meaningful; and the CPU-only production
environment makes sequence model inference at district scale
impractical. The spatial robustness experiment showed that the
QUADKEY-level macro F1 of 0.9305 is an optimistic upper bound
inflated by spatial autocorrelation, and that the field/group
split produces a more conservative estimate of 0.81--0.82.

The Nyagatare inference was assessed through visual spatial
plausibility and cross-check against the NISR SAS Season~B~2025
report. MAIZE dominance was confirmed as directionally consistent
with official characterisation of the district. The bean
underestimation was explained as a measurement mismatch between
farmer-reported cultivated area and pixel-level spectral
classification in a landscape where 75.9\% of cultivated area
is intercropped --- a finding with broader implications for
how satellite-derived crop maps should be compared against
area-based agricultural statistics in intercropped systems.
The uncertain prediction category was identified as a
transparency feature providing downstream users with a spatial
quality indicator.

The work is positioned as the foundational spatial layer of
Oracle's Government Data Intelligence for Agriculture platform,
