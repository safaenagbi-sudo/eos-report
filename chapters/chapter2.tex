\section{Introduction}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────

This chapter reviews the theoretical foundations and existing literature relevant 
to crop mapping using satellite imagery and machine learning techniques. 
It begins by introducing the principles of remote sensing and the 
characteristics of Sentinel-2 imagery that make it suitable for agricultural 
applications. It then examines crop phenology as a key concept underlying 
time-series-based classification. 

Finally, it reviews existing approaches to crop mapping from snapshot-based methods to time-series and deep learning 
techniques identifying their strengths, limitations, and the gaps that 
motivate the approach adopted in this project.

\section{Remote Sensing Fundamentals}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────
Remote sensing refers to the acquisition of information about an object, surface, or phenomenon without direct physical contact.
In Earth Observation, remote sensing systems mounted on satellites or aircraft measure the electromagnetic radiation reflected 
or emitted by the Earth's surface in order to characterize natural and human activities. 

Remote sensing technologies have become fundamental tools for environmental monitoring, land-cover analysis, climate studies, and agricultural management.
By providing repeated and large-scale observations of the Earth's surface, satellite imagery enables continuous monitoring of vegetation 
dynamics and crop development over time.

  % ── 2.2.1 ──
  \subsection{Electromagnetic Spectrum}

  Electromagnetic radiation is a form of energy that propagates through space in the form of waves.
  The electromagnetic spectrum encompasses a wide range of wavelengths, extending from gamma rays and X-rays to visible
  light, infrared, and radio waves.
  
  Remote sensing applications primarily rely on the \textbf{visible} , \textbf{near-infrared} (NIR), 
  and \textbf{shortwave infrared} (SWIR) portions of the spectrum, as these bands contain valuable information about vegetation 
  and soil characteristics.
  
  \begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/Electromagnetic-Spectrum.png}
    \caption{Electromagnetic spectrum}
    
  \end{figure}

  \textbf{Visible light} corresponds to wavelengths approximately between 400~nm and 700~nm and includes the blue,
  green, and red bands perceived by the human eye. Vegetation strongly absorbs red light due to chlorophyll activity while reflecting
  green light, which explains the green appearance of plants. 
  
  \textbf{Near-infrared} radiation, typically between 700~nm and 1300~nm,
  is highly reflected by healthy vegetation because of the internal cellular structure of plant leaves. This property makes the NIR region
  particularly important for vegetation monitoring and crop analysis. 
  
  \textbf{Shortwave infrared} bands are sensitive to vegetation moisture content,
  soil properties, and water stress conditions. These bands are frequently used in agricultural and environmental monitoring applications.

  The spectral response of different land-cover types varies across wavelengths, allowing satellite sensors to distinguish vegetation, water bodies,
  urban areas, and bare soil based on their spectral signatures.

  

  % ── 2.2.2 ──
  \subsection{Interaction with Earth's Surface}

  When electromagnetic radiation reaches the Earth's surface, it is 
  either \textbf{absorbed}, \textbf{transmitted}, or \textbf{reflected}. The proportion of each 
  depends on the wavelength and the physical and chemical properties 
  of the surface material. This wavelength-dependent reflectance 
  behaviour is referred to as a \textbf{spectral signature}, and it forms the 
  physical basis for distinguishing different land cover types from 
  satellite imagery~\cite{jensen2016}.

  Vegetation exhibits a distinctive spectral signature: strong 
  absorption in the red band (around 670~nm) driven by chlorophyll 
  activity, and high reflectance in the near-infrared (NIR) region 
  (around 800~nm) due to the internal structure of leaf cells. Bare 
  soil, water, and built surfaces each exhibit markedly different 
  signatures, enabling automated classification algorithms to 
  discriminate between them. Within vegetation itself, spectral 
  differences between crop types, though subtler, can be 
  exploited when sufficient spectral and temporal resolution is 
  available \cite{thenkabail2018}.

  % ── 2.2.3 ──
  \subsection{Remote Sensing Applications in Agriculture}
   
  Remote sensing has become an essential technology for modern \textbf{agricultural
  monitoring} and \textbf{precision farming}. Satellite imagery enables large-scale 
  and frequent observation of agricultural areas without the need for costly field surveys.

  In agriculture, remote sensing is commonly used for \textbf{crop type mapping}, 
  \textbf{vegetation health assessment}, \textbf{yield estimation}, \textbf{drought monitoring},
  \textbf{irrigation management}, and detection of environmental \textbf{stress conditions}. 
   
  The temporal dimension provided by satellite time series is particularly important 
  for crop classification tasks. Different crops exhibit distinct seasonal growth patterns,
  which can be observed through changes in spectral reflectance over time. Monitoring these 
  temporal dynamics enables more accurate identification of crop types compared to single-date observations.

  Despite its advantages, agricultural remote sensing also faces several challenges. Persistent cloud cover
  may reduce image availability, especially in tropical regions. Mixed pixels caused by 
  fragmented agricultural parcels can introduce spectral ambiguity, while variations in planting dates 
  and farming practices increase the complexity of crop classification tasks.

\section{Sentinel-2 Satellite Imagery}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────
  
  Sentinel-2 is one of the most important Earth Observation missions developed within the \textbf{European Union's Copernicus Programme}.
   specifically designed for land monitoring applications. Its high temporal resolution, 
  rich spectral information, and free open-access policy make it a de facto 
  standard for large-scale crop classification tasks based on satellite 
  time series.

  % ── 2.3.1 ──
  \subsection{Overview of the Sentinel-2 Mission}

  The Sentinel-2 mission consists of two polar-orbiting satellites: 
  \textbf{Sentinel-2A}, launched in 2015, and \textbf{Sentinel-2B}, launched in 2017. 
  Both satellites operate in the same sun-synchronous orbit at an 
  altitude of approximately 786~km. The combination of the two 
  satellites enables a revisit frequency of approximately \textbf{five days}
  at the equator and even shorter revisit times at higher latitudes. 
  This frequent acquisition capability is essential for monitoring 
  dynamic agricultural processes and capturing crop evolution 
  throughout the growing season \cite{drusch2012}.

  Sentinel-2 provides \textbf{multispectral imagery} in thirteen spectral 
  bands covering the visible, near-infrared (NIR), and shortwave 
  infrared (SWIR) regions of the electromagnetic spectrum, at 
  spatial resolutions of \textbf{10~m, 20~m, and 60~m} depending on 
  the spectral band. Its free and open-access policy has 
  significantly accelerated research and operational applications 
  in remote sensing and agricultural monitoring worldwide.

  \subsection{Spectral Bands and Agricultural Applications}
  
  The Sentinel-2 sensor acquires data in \textbf{thirteen spectral} bands specifically designed
  for vegetation, soil, and water monitoring applications. These bands capture different 
  physical and biochemical properties of vegetation and are widely used for crop analysis.

  The \textbf{visible bands} (Blue, Green, and Red) are mainly used for visual interpretation and vegetation analysis.
  The \textbf{near-infrared band} is highly sensitive to plant vigor and biomass, while the \textbf{red-edge} bands provide valuable
  information about chlorophyll concentration and vegetation stress. The \textbf{shortwave infrared} bands are useful for monitoring 
  moisture content and soil conditions.

  % ── 2.3.3 ──
  \subsection{Spatial Indexing and Data Organisation}
    Efficient spatial organisation of satellite imagery is essential 
  for large-scale time-series analysis. Two complementary indexing 
  systems are relevant to this project: the MGRS tile grid used 
  natively by Sentinel-2, and the quadkey hierarchical indexing 
  scheme used for cloud-based data management.
   
    \subsubsection{The Sentinel-2 Tile System and MGRS Grid}

      Sentinel-2 imagery is organised and distributed using the 
    \textbf{Military Grid Reference System} (MGRS), a global spatial 
    indexing framework built on top of the \textbf{Universal Transverse 
    Mercator} (UTM) projection \cite{esa2015}. The UTM system 
    divides the Earth into 60 longitudinal zones of 6 degrees 
    each, numbered 1 to 60 from west to east. Within each zone, 
    coordinates are expressed in metres, easting and northing,
    relative to a zone-specific central meridian, minimising 
    distortion within each zone and ensuring geometric consistency 
    for pixel-level spatial operations.

    Under the MGRS system, each UTM zone is further subdivided 
    into a grid of 100~$\times$~100~km tiles, each identified 
    by a unique alphanumeric code composed of the UTM zone number, 
    a latitude band letter, and a two-letter square identifier,
    for example, \texttt{36MTC} and \texttt{36MTD} for tiles 
    covering Rwanda \cite{esa2015}. Each Sentinel-2 acquisition 
    is projected and delivered within this fixed tile grid, 
    ensuring that imagery from different dates is inherently 
    co-registered and spatially aligned, a prerequisite for 
    constructing pixel-wise time series.

    \begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/MGRS_GZD.png}
    \caption{ Military Grid Reference System showing the 6° x 8° grid cells  }
    \label{fig:MGRS}
    \end{figure}

    \subsubsection{Quadkey Hierarchical Indexing}

    Beyond the MGRS tile system, modern large-scale geospatial 
    platforms increasingly rely on hierarchical spatial indexing 
    schemes to efficiently store, query, and retrieve satellite 
    data at scale. One of the most widely adopted schemes is 
    the \textbf{quadkey system}, originally introduced by Microsoft for 
    web mapping applications \cite{openstreetmap2023}.

    The quadkey system subdivides the Earth's surface recursively 
    into a quadtree of tiles at increasing zoom levels. At each 
    level, every tile is divided into four equal quadrants: 
    top-left (0), top-right (1), bottom-left (2), and 
    bottom-right (3). A tile at any zoom level is uniquely 
    identified by a string of digits encoding its hierarchical 
    position. For example, a tile at zoom level 3 might carry 
    the quadkey \texttt{021}, meaning it occupies the second 
    quadrant of the first quadrant of the third quadrant of 
    the root tile.
    
    \begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.74\textwidth]{figures/Quadkeys.png}
    \caption{Quadkey hierarchical tile subdivision at increasing zoom levels.}
    \label{fig:quadkey}
    \end{figure}

    This recursive structure offers several practical advantages 
    for large-scale geospatial data management. The multi-resolution hierarchy 
    also naturally supports data aggregation across spatial 
    scales \cite{openstreetmap2023}. In the context of this 
    project, quadkey indexing is used to partition Sentinel-2 
    time series data enabling 
    efficient tile-based processing and scalable retrieval 
    of pixel observations across Rwanda's territory.

\section{Crop Phenology}
% ============================================================

Crop phenology refers to the study of cyclic and seasonal biological 
events in crops and their timing in relation to climate and 
environmental conditions \cite{de2017}. From a remote sensing 
perspective, phenology is the key signal that allows satellite 
time series to discriminate between crop types that may appear 
spectrally similar at any single point in time but follow 
distinctly different growth trajectories over a full growing 
season.

  % ── 2.4.1 ──
  \subsection{Phenological Cycles and Temporal Profiles}
  Each crop type follows a characteristic growth cycle, from 
  germination and early vegetative growth, through canopy closure, 
  flowering, and grain fill, to senescence and harvest. This cycle 
  produces a distinctive temporal profile of spectral reflectance 
  when observed repeatedly from satellite, commonly referred to 
  as a phenological curve \cite{inglada2016}.

  These temporal profiles are the fundamental signal exploited 
  by time-series classification approaches. Crops that are 
  spectrally similar at a single date may exhibit clearly 
  distinct seasonal trajectories, differing in the timing 
  of green-up, peak greenness, amplitude, and senescence date 
  making temporal information essential for accurate 
  multi-class crop mapping \cite{defourny2019}. Key phenological 
  metrics such as start of season, end of season, peak greenness, 
  seasonal amplitude, and seasonal integral serve as compact 
  and discriminative features encoding crop-type-specific 
  growth dynamics.

  % ── 2.4.2 ──
  \subsection{Crop Calendar in Rwanda}

  Agriculture in Rwanda is primarily organised around two major 
  cropping seasons, commonly referred to as \textbf{Season A} and \textbf{Season B}, 
  strongly influenced by rainfall patterns and climatic conditions 
  \cite{minagri2018}. Season A generally begins in September and 
  ends in February, while Season B extends from February to August. 
  Certain regions also support an additional short agricultural 
  season known as Season C, typically practised in irrigated 
  areas between July and August.

  The temporal distribution of planting and harvesting periods 
  varies across crop types and geographical regions, reflecting 
  Rwanda's diverse agro-ecological zones. Major crops cultivated 
  in Rwanda include \textbf{maize, beans, cassava, Irish potato, rice, 
  banana, and sweet potato} \cite{minagri2018}. Understanding 
  the crop calendar is essential for interpreting satellite 
  time-series data, as crop spectral responses are directly 
  linked to their phenological development stages. The overlap 
  between rainy seasons and cropping periods also introduces 
  persistent cloud cover, creating temporal gaps in Sentinel-2 
  observations that must be addressed in the preprocessing 
  pipeline.

\section{Literature Review}
% ============================================================

Crop type mapping using satellite imagery has been an active 
area of research over the past two decades, driven by advances 
in satellite data availability, machine learning, and cloud 
computing. This section reviews the existing literature 
relevant to this project, organised around three major 
themes: snapshot-based classification approaches, 
time-series-based methods, and the specific challenges 
of rare class detection and imbalanced learning in 
agricultural remote sensing. For each theme, key 
contributions are synthesised, their limitations 
identified, and their relevance to the approach 
adopted in this project discussed.


  % ── 2.5.1 ──
  \subsection{Snapshot-Based Approaches}
  Early work in satellite-based crop mapping relied predominantly 
  on single-date or snapshot imagery, where classification is 
  performed using spectral information from one or a small number 
  of acquisition dates. While computationally straightforward, 
  these approaches are fundamentally limited by the fact that 
  a single image captures only one moment in a crop's growth 
  cycle, making it difficult to distinguish spectrally similar 
  crops that differ primarily in their temporal behaviour.

    \subsubsection{Traditional Machine Learning and Ensemble Methods}
    Traditional machine learning methods, particularly \textbf{Support 
    Vector Machines} (SVM), \textbf{Random Forest} (RF), and \textbf{gradient 
    boosting} approaches, have been widely applied to crop 
    mapping using single-date or multi-date optical imagery 
    \cite{mountrakis2011}. These methods operate on handcrafted 
    spectral features derived from individual bands or vegetation 
    indices, and have demonstrated competitive performance in 
    regions with simple cropping systems and low cloud cover.

    \textbf{Random Forest} in particular has become a standard baseline 
    for crop classification tasks due to its robustness to 
    noisy features, ability to handle high-dimensional inputs, 
    and built-in feature importance estimation \cite{breiman2001}. 
    \textbf{Gradient boosting} methods such as \textbf{XGBoost} have more recently 
    demonstrated superior performance in tabular classification 
    tasks, achieving state-of-the-art results on several 
    remote sensing benchmarks \cite{chen2016}. However, both 
    approaches applied to single-date imagery are inherently 
    limited: they cannot capture the temporal dynamics that 
    distinguish crop types with similar spectral signatures 
    at any given moment.

    \subsubsection{Deep Learning Methods for Spatial Classification}
    
    The advent of deep learning brought convolutional neural 
    networks (CNNs) to remote sensing, enabling the automatic 
    extraction of spatial features from satellite imagery without 
    manual feature engineering \cite{lecun2015}. CNN-based 
    approaches applied to single-date imagery have shown strong 
    performance in land cover mapping tasks, particularly when 
    large labelled datasets are available \cite{maggiori2017}.

    For crop mapping specifically, CNNs have been applied to 
    high-resolution imagery to exploit spatial context, including
    field shape, texture, and neighbourhood relationships 
    as complementary discriminative information beyond 
    spectral signatures alone \cite{kussul2017}. Despite 
    these advances, CNN-based snapshot approaches share 
    the same fundamental limitation as traditional methods: 
    they remain blind to the temporal dimension of crop 
    growth, which is often the most discriminative signal 
    for crop type identification.


    \subsubsection{Challenges and Limitations}

    Snapshot-based approaches face several inherent limitations 
    that restrict their applicability in complex agricultural 
    contexts such as Rwanda. First, the reliance on a single 
    acquisition date introduces high sensitivity to atmospheric 
    conditions, cloud cover, and phenological variability 
    between years, meaning a single cloudy image can render an 
    entire classification pipeline unusable \cite{inglada2016}. 
    Second, spectrally similar crops that differ primarily 
    in their growth timing cannot be reliably distinguished 
    from a single observation. Third, the class imbalance 
    typical of smallholder landscapes, where dominant 
    crops vastly outnumber minority crops, is particularly 
    difficult to address without the additional discriminative 
    power provided by temporal information. These limitations 
    motivated a shift toward time-series-based approaches.

  % ── 2.5.2 ── 
  \subsection{Time-Series Based Approaches}
  The availability of dense satellite time series, made 
  possible by missions such as Sentinel-2, has enabled 
  a fundamental shift in crop mapping methodology. Rather 
  than relying on spectral snapshots, time-series approaches 
  exploit the full temporal trajectory of crop growth, 
  capturing phenological dynamics that are highly 
  discriminative across crop types \cite{inglada2016}.


  \subsubsection{Classical Time-Series Classification}

    Early time-series classification methods for crop mapping 
    were based on template matching and distance-based 
    approaches. \textbf{Dynamic Time Warping} (DTW), which measures 
    similarity between temporal sequences by allowing 
    elastic alignment in time, was among the first methods 
    applied to satellite phenological curves for crop 
    discrimination \cite{maus2016}. While effective for 
    simple cropping systems, DTW-based methods scale poorly 
    to large datasets and struggle with noisy or 
    gap-filled time series.

    \textbf{Threshold-based} phenological methods, which classify 
    pixels based on the timing and magnitude of key 
    phenological events such as start of season, peak, 
    and end of season, have also been widely used 
    for crop mapping in data-sparse environments 
    \cite{barcanibar2020}. These approaches are 
    interpretable and computationally efficient but 
    require careful calibration and are sensitive 
    to temporal gaps introduced by cloud cover.

    \subsubsection{Deep Learning for Time-Series: LSTM and GRU}

    \textbf{Recurrent neural networks (RNNs)}, and in particular 
    \textbf{Long Short-Term Memory} (LSTM) networks and \textbf{Gated 
    Recurrent Units} (GRUs), have emerged as the dominant 
    deep learning architectures for satellite time-series 
    classification \cite{hochreiter1997}. Unlike classical 
    methods, these architectures learn temporal dependencies 
    directly from data, without requiring explicit 
    phenological feature engineering.

    \textcite{russwurm2017} were among the first to apply 
    LSTM networks to Sentinel-2 time series for crop 
    mapping, demonstrating significant improvements over 
    Random Forest baselines on European agricultural 
    landscapes. Subsequent studies confirmed that 
    recurrent architectures consistently outperform 
    classical machine learning methods when sufficient 
    temporal observations are available, particularly 
    for minority crop classes with distinctive but 
    short-duration phenological signals \cite{interdonato2019}.

    GRU-based models have been shown to achieve performance 
    comparable to LSTM while requiring fewer parameters 
    and training time, making them particularly attractive 
    for large-scale operational applications 
    \cite{cho2014}. More recently, attention-based 
    mechanisms and transformer architectures have 
    been explored for satellite time-series 
    classification, though their advantage over 
    well-tuned recurrent models remains dataset-dependent 
    \cite{garnot2020}.

    \subsubsection{Phenology-Based Temporal Classification}

    A complementary line of research has focused on 
    extracting explicit phenological features from 
    satellite time series, such as start of season, 
    end of season, peak greenness, seasonal amplitude, 
    and seasonal integral, and using these as inputs 
    to classical machine learning classifiers 
    \cite{inglada2016}. This hybrid approach combines 
    the interpretability of phenological metrics with 
    the discriminative power of supervised learning, 
    and has demonstrated strong performance in 
    smallholder contexts where training data is 
    limited \cite{defourny2019}.

    Studies applying this approach to sub-Saharan 
    African agricultural landscapes have highlighted 
    the importance of carefully handling temporal 
    gaps introduced by cloud cover before extracting 
    phenological features, as gap-filling quality 
    directly affects the reliability of derived 
    metrics \cite{rs2020rwanda}.

  % ── 2.5.3 ── 
  \subsection{Comparative Analysis and Positioning of Our Work}

  The reviewed literature highlights a clear evolution in crop 
  type mapping methodology, from simple snapshot-based 
  approaches relying on single-date spectral information, 
  to sophisticated time-series methods that exploit the 
  full temporal trajectory of crop growth. While significant 
  progress has been made, several challenges remain 
  insufficiently addressed, particularly in the context 
  of smallholder agriculture in sub-Saharan Africa.

  Most existing studies are conducted in temperate agricultural 
  landscapes characterised by large, regular fields and 
  relatively low cloud cover. Few works explicitly address 
  the combination of challenges present in Rwanda: a bimodal 
  cropping calendar, persistent cloud cover during both 
  growing seasons, extreme field fragmentation, and severe 
  class imbalance between dominant and minority crops.

  Furthermore, the majority of published pipelines report 
  overall accuracy metrics that are dominated by majority 
  classes, masking poor performance on rare crop types. 
  The challenge of detecting minority crops, which are 
  often the most important from a food security perspective 
  remains an open problem that receives limited 
  attention in the literature.

  This project is positioned at the intersection of these 
  gaps. It targets a real-world, national-scale crop 
  mapping problem in Rwanda, explicitly addressing the 
  challenges of cloud cover, class imbalance, and rare 
  crop detection within a single end-to-end pipeline. 
  By comparing both classical machine learning and deep 
  learning approaches on the same dataset and evaluation 
  framework, it contributes a rigorous and reproducible 
  benchmark for crop mapping in challenging smallholder 
  contexts.

\section{Conclusion}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────

This chapter presented the theoretical foundations and 
existing literature relevant to crop type mapping using 
Sentinel-2 satellite time series. The first part introduced 
the principles of remote sensing, the characteristics 
of Sentinel-2 imagery, and the role of crop phenology 
as the key temporal signal for crop discrimination. 
the second part reviewed existing approaches to crop mapping, 
from snapshot-based methods to time-series and deep 
learning techniques, identifying their strengths and 
limitations.

The review identified three key gaps in the existing 
literature: insufficient attention to rare class 
detection, the widespread neglect of spatial leakage 
through boundary pixels, and a lack of studies 
addressing Rwanda's specific agricultural context. 
The methodology presented in the following chapter 
is designed to address each of these gaps through 
a rigorous, end-to-end crop classification pipeline 
built on Sentinel-2 time series data processed on 
Oracle Cloud Infrastructure.
